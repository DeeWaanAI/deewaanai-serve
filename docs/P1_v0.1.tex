\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language models are attractive on consumer hardware because
each token activates only a small slice of the weights, yet the full weight set must
still be resident or streamed. We study \emph{pinned-expert orchestration}---keeping a
traffic-ranked subset of experts resident in unified memory, dispatching via a gather
kernel, and streaming cold experts from disk with continuous batching---against the
de-facto local-inference incumbent, llama.cpp, on Apple Silicon. We report a single
controlled study read at opposite ends of the device-RAM-to-model-size ratio, using a
tail-latency metric (the 5th-percentile per-user decode throughput, p05, against a
\SI{10}{tok/s} service-level floor) and same-machine uncensored concurrency ceilings.
In the fits-RAM regime, pinning serves up to \textbf{$2\ppl$ the concurrent users} of
llama.cpp, but \emph{only} when the model is a small fraction of RAM: a $2.0\ppl$ win at
a $3.8\ppl$ RAM/4-bit-GB ratio (OLMoE-1B-7B, 16 vs.\ 8 users), reversing to $0.5\ppl$ at
$1.8\ppl$ (Qwen1.5-MoE) and to a $1.0\ppl$ tie at $0.9\ppl$ (Qwen3-30B-A3B). Router
telemetry explains the bound: cross-request overlap is high (median pairwise Jaccard
$0.593$), the resident expert set grows sub-linearly with concurrency (36.4 to $56.2$ of
64 experts for 95\% batch coverage), and a single request already touches
$45.2/64=70.6\%$ of the pool---there is no small hot set to pin, capping the win near
$2\ppl$ and collapsing it as coverage exceeds RAM. In the beyond-RAM regime we find a
\emph{feasibility} rather than speed result: llama.cpp's Metal path cannot load an 18\,GB
model on a 16\,GB Mac (GPU out-of-memory), whereas our engine streams it at
${\approx}\SI{2.1}{tok/s}$. Streaming is a cliff, not a slope---only full residency is
usable without assistance---but within-step \emph{parallel} input/output prefetch turns
the cliff into a slope ($2.1\ppl$ single-user speedup on the real >RAM model). Two
hypotheses are refuted on single-SSD consumer hardware: \emph{predictive} prefetch
($0.372$ vs.\ $0.909$ tok/s serial) and trading expert residency for key-value capacity
(p05 ceiling drops $12\!\to\!4$). We position the system against the 2025--26
working-set and prefetch literature and state plainly which mechanisms we have
implemented and which we have not. A prior 24-hour campaign on a rented 24\,GB Mac
(Section~\ref{sec:gate1}) established that streaming preserves output quality (perplexity
within $0.41\%$ once a kernel-parity confound was fixed), located the single-user ceiling
in memory-read bandwidth, and ruled out a custom-kernel path---redirecting the programme
to orchestration. Every number traces to a frozen artifact via a
committed, self-verifying extraction script.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Mixture-of-Experts models decouple parameter count from per-token compute: a
$30$B-parameter model may activate only $3$B parameters per token. On memory-constrained
consumer hardware---laptops and small servers with unified memory and a single NVMe
drive---this sparsity is the only reason a large model can be served at all. The
practical question for a systems builder is not ``can it fit'' but ``how many
concurrent users can it serve, and can it serve a model that does not fit?''

We built a \emph{pinned-expert orchestration engine} on top of the stock
\texttt{mlx\_lm} model implementation for Apple Silicon: we swap only the
MoE feed-forward dispatch, keeping attention, embeddings, and the router untouched, and
compare against llama.cpp (\texttt{llama-server}), the incumbent tool that practitioners
actually run. Our evaluation is deliberately conservative: a per-user tail statistic
(p05) rather than an aggregate, same-machine ratios rather than absolute throughput,
uncensored ceilings (run to failure) rather than the highest level we happened to test,
and fidelity gates passed before any concurrency claim is trusted.

The paper makes four contributions, each proven in the body and traceable in
Appendix~A.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{A measured crossover curve} (Section~\ref{sec:crossover}) for MoE serving
        on M-series unified memory under a per-user SLO floor, spanning three current
        models and $3.8\ppl$ to $0.9\ppl$ RAM-to-model ratios. To our knowledge this
        crossover is not reported in the prior literature (Section~\ref{sec:related}).
  \item \textbf{A measured mechanism} (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}) for the bound: the
        sub-linear growth of the resident expert set amortises across concurrent users,
        while routing skew caps the win near $2\ppl$.
  \item \textbf{A >RAM feasibility result} (Section~\ref{sec:beyond}), together with the
        $2.1\ppl$ within-step parallel-prefetch win that makes streaming usable.
  \item \textbf{Three disciplined negative results}---predictive prefetch,
        residency-for-KV eviction, and a custom Python/MLX gather kernel
        (Appendix~C)---reported as negatives, plus the methodological spine
        (Section~\ref{sec:method}) that keeps them honest.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:gate1} first reports the exploratory rental campaign that generated these
hypotheses; Sections~\ref{sec:crossover} and~\ref{sec:beyond} then test them under tighter
control. All concurrency results---rental and laptop---are reported on one p05 statistic.

\paragraph{Scope and non-claims.} We do \emph{not} claim single-stream speed superiority
(llama.cpp is faster per user on Qwen3-30B). We do \emph{not} claim a concurrency win in
the >RAM regime (both engines are below the SLO floor there; the claim is feasibility).
We report same-machine \emph{ratios}; absolute tok/s differ across chips. The custom
gather kernel is a negative result, not the mechanism behind our wins, which come from
the standard \texttt{mx.gather\_qmm} path.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Working-set and memory-bounded MoE serving.} A 2025--26 line treats MoE
inference as a bounded-working-set problem. WiSP (arXiv:2606.21868) analyses
low-resource MoE serving and shows static layer offload forfeits the sparsity advantage;
MawForge (arXiv:2607.09686) keeps common tensors resident and materialises routed
experts into a bounded cache; fine-grained expert offloading targets the
latency--memory trade-off (arXiv:2502.05370); and DuoServe-MoE (arXiv:2509.07379)
splits a dense-prefill / sparse-decode schedule. Our fits-RAM crossover is the
empirical, ratio-swept complement that these designs assume but rarely report.

\paragraph{Predictive prefetch and scheduling.} SpecPrefetch (arXiv:2607.24787)
predicts next-layer experts with a parameter-efficient head while keeping the native
router authoritative---exactly the safety property we adopt for our (non-predictive)
prefetch. ExpertFlow (DAC'26) and Edge0 are industry/open-source systems reporting large
speedups and sub-RAM serving of $35$B models; we cite them as design points, flagging
that no confirmed arXiv identifier is available for ExpertFlow and that Edge0 is a
project rather than a paper. Klotski (arXiv:2502.06888) groups rows that share experts
to amortise I/O. Section~\ref{sec:beyond} reports our careful negative: on a single SSD,
\emph{prediction} does not pay because \emph{parallelism} already captures the overlap.

\paragraph{Cache-aware routing.} Mixture of Cache-Conditional Experts
(arXiv:2412.00099) retrains the router to prefer resident experts; ``Cacheable by
Design?'' (arXiv:2608.18261) is a pre-registered \emph{negative} showing a batch
scheme collapsing at batch~32 from paging thrash. We implement neither cache-aware
routing nor token scheduling; our engine is closer to the baseline these systems
improve on.

\paragraph{KV--expert contention.} The \emph{qs} inequality (arXiv:2603.08960)
formalises the double penalty we observe empirically---routing fragments reuse, and a
large resident expert pool starves KV headroom. It is the natural frame for our
OLMoE-win / Qwen-loss swing and, in Section~\ref{sec:beyond}, for why trading experts
for KV backfires.

\paragraph{Measurement.} Tail-fairness work (FairBatching arXiv:2510.14392, Niyama
arXiv:2503.22762, SLO-aware sliding-window arXiv:2606.05933) motivates reporting a
low-percentile ceiling rather than an aggregate mean. Roofline analyses
(arXiv:2605.30571) warn that batch-1 decode is memory-bound but not purely
bandwidth-limited; we therefore compare engines to each other, not to theoretical peak.

\paragraph{Apple-Silicon-specific.} NPU MoE serving (arXiv:2604.18788) and multi-node
expert parallelism (arXiv:2506.23635) characterise M-series inference. None reports a
RAM/model-size crossover curve or a >RAM concurrency/feasibility result on M-series
unified memory under a per-user SLO floor---the gap this paper owns.

\section{System, Metric, and Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{System under test}
The backbone is a stock \texttt{mlx\_lm} model; we replace only the MoE block
(\texttt{switch\_mlp}/SwitchGLU) with a \texttt{PinnedSwitchMLP}: a resident stack of
hot experts plus a gather-QMM dispatch, with cold experts streamed from per-expert
on-disk ``slabs.'' The MLX 4-bit checkpoints already store routed experts in the
engine's layout (packed uint32 with float scales and biases, group~64, affine), so slabs
are extracted \emph{verbatim} with no re-quantisation (\texttt{extract\_mlx\_experts.py}).
Continuous batching uses a ``world-coordinate'' rope-shift trick that lets multiple
sequences share one batch without altering relative positions. The baseline is
\texttt{llama-server}.

\subsection{Metric: p05 concurrency ceiling}
Our primary metric is the \textbf{p05 concurrency ceiling}: the largest number of
simultaneous users for which the 5th-percentile per-user decode throughput remains at or
above a \SI{10}{tok/s} floor. We choose p05 over the minimum (a single straggler request
mis-ranks arms) and over the median (which overstates the tail). Percentiles use linear
interpolation (numpy-equivalent). Ceilings are \textbf{uncensored}: every arm is run to
failure so a ceiling is never truncated by the top tested level. All arms on a machine
share an identical per-user context budget, identical prompts and repetitions, and the
same quantisation; llama.cpp is given enough parallel slots that it is not artificially
queue-capped.

\subsection{Fidelity gates}
Before any ceiling is trusted we require: (i)~\emph{synthetic equivalence}---the
\texttt{PinnedSwitchMLP} reproduces stock \texttt{SwitchGLU} bit-exactly at each model's
dimensions in decode and prefill regimes (\texttt{switch\_equiv\_gate.py}); (ii)~\emph{end-to-end greedy
match}---stock generation versus pinned-fully-resident is token-identical on real weights
(\texttt{lfm2\_e2e\_equiv.py}); and (iii)~a \emph{perplexity gate} on the frozen corpus,
reported in Section~\ref{sec:fidelity}. Prefetch correctness is additionally gated to a
byte-identical SHA (\texttt{prefetch\_equiv\_test.py}).

\subsection{Corpus and reproducibility}
Traces and perplexity use a frozen WildChat-3k subset (SHA-256 recorded in
\texttt{results/corpus}). Raw run outputs are hash-frozen (\texttt{FROZEN\_MANIFEST.json}).
The paper's quantitative spine is produced by a single committed, self-verifying script
(\texttt{code/paper/extract\_headline\_numbers.py}) that reads only frozen files and
emits \texttt{results/derived/paper\_headline\_numbers.json}; a $52$-entry decision log
(\texttt{DECISIONS.md}) records every design and correction (Appendix~A).

\subsection{Harness validation (Stage-0)}
The harness itself is validated before use: on the resident reference configuration the
prompt-processing rate is $1261.4\pm39.5$ tok/s and generation is $132.1\pm0.2$ tok/s
against a $\ge 66.0$ gate, with $5/5$ arm parity---establishing that later concurrency
differences are attributable to the engine under test, not to measurement noise.

\section{Gate-1: Exploratory Campaign on a Rented 24 GB Mac}
\label{sec:gate1}

Before the two controlled laptop studies we ran a 24-hour exploratory campaign on a
rented AWS Mac (M4, 24\,GB unified memory) to learn the shape of the problem. It is a
\emph{hypothesis-generating} study, not a confirmation. Its concurrency ceilings are
re-scored here under the \emph{same} p05 rule used everywhere else in this paper (raw
request logs preserved), so the whole paper reports one statistic across both machines;
where the original on-box write-ups differed, we defer to the re-scored data. The
campaign's lasting value is in results a 16\,GB laptop cannot reproduce.

\subsection{Multi-user serving across three models (v1)}
Table~\ref{tab:gate1} gives the rental ceilings. OLMoE, which fits with headroom, is the
first win: 12 users versus llama.cpp's 8, with a far tighter tail (the p05 user still
gets $25.4$ tok/s at 8 concurrent versus $10.8$ for llama.cpp). Qwen3-30B-A3B, near
capacity, is the first honest \emph{loss}: both arms ceiling at 4, but llama.cpp is
${\approx}1.9\ppl$ faster per single user ($43.6$ vs.\ $23.2$ tok/s). Moonlight-16B
(DeepSeek lineage) uses traditional RoPE, which our batch-attach path does not support;
the engine \emph{refuses to run} rather than risk silently wrong output, so there is no
contest on that model.

\subsection{Two speed attempts, both null (v2, v3)}
We tried to close the single-user gap two ways and neither worked. Compiling the expert
math with \texttt{mx.compile} (v2) produced bit-exact output but a $\le 2\%$ performance
change---a null (D-024). Capping prefill bursts to protect the tail (v3) left the p05
ceiling unchanged at 12, identical to v1---also a null on natural traffic. These negatives
matter: they told us the gap was not software-language overhead, motivating the
attribution below.

\subsection{Routing is nearly flat (the headliner finding)}
Expert-activation traces on the frozen corpus showed routing is close to uniform: mean
Zipf $\alpha\approx0.44$ and the top-10 experts carry only 15--30\% of visits. This
weakened the ``pin a small hot set'' premise and is the mechanistic reason the crossover
advantage is bounded near $2\ppl$ (quantified independently on the laptop in
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).

\subsection{Streaming preserves quality --- after we fixed a confound}
\label{sec:gate1ppl}
We validated streamed cold experts against resident ones with a perplexity gate
($|\Delta\text{PPL}|/\text{PPL}_{\text{stock}} \le 0.5\%$ at \emph{every} residency
fraction). The \emph{first} run \textbf{failed}, and the failure was informative: the
cold-stream path used a \emph{different math kernel} than the resident stack, so the
perplexity drifted ($0.69\%$ at half residency). Restoring kernel parity (D-029
$\rightarrow$ D-032) turned the gate green (Table~\ref{tab:ppl}): the maximum relative
change is $0.41\%$ at 10\% residency, improving monotonically to $0.09\%$ at 80\% and
exactly $0.00\%$ at full residency. Two notes of honesty: this gate was run on a model
that \emph{fits} memory (varying residency via pin-fraction), so it establishes
streamed-equals-resident equivalence rather than >RAM fidelity; and the earlier
perplexity breach is retained above as the caught confound, not quietly dropped.

\subsection{The bottleneck is memory reads, not the language (v2/v3 aftermath)}
\label{sec:gate1diag}
A decode-attribution probe on Qwen3-30B put a full step at $43.4$\,ms/token, split
${\approx}66\%$ expert compute, ${\approx}20\%$ attention, and ${\approx}8\%$ host/Python
overhead---ruling out the interpreter as the lever. A bandwidth microbenchmark measured
the gather path at ${\approx}10.6$\,GB/s on the intended-bytes basis, well short of the
chip's ceiling. The tempting fix---a hand-written vectorised gather---proved \textbf{33\ppl
slower} (Appendix~C): kernel fusion, not Python-level vectorisation, governs the per-token
floor. That cheap measurement redirected the whole programme away from custom kernels and
toward \emph{orchestration}, which is what the two controlled studies that follow test.

\paragraph{From Gate-1 to this paper.} The rental gave the crossover its first shape
(a win on the fitting model, a tie/loss on the larger one), the quality gate, the
flat-routing mechanism, and the bandwidth verdict. It left the concurrency ceiling
imprecise and the >RAM regime untested; Sections~\ref{sec:crossover} and~\ref{sec:beyond}
supply the uncensored p05 crossover, the >RAM feasibility result, and the corrected
numbers, on one statistic throughout.

\subsection{Result}
Pinning wins---but only at high ratio. On OLMoE ($3.8\ppl$) it doubles the concurrent
users (16 vs.\ 8). By $1.8\ppl$ (Qwen1.5-MoE) the advantage is gone and mildly reverses
(4 vs.\ 8); at $0.9\ppl$ (Qwen3-30B) both arms ceiling at 4. Figure~\ref{fig:curve}
summarises advantage against ratio.

The per-user detail (Table~\ref{tab:olmoelevels}) shows the signature: llama.cpp is
roughly $2\ppl$ faster per user at 1--2 users (p05 64.7 vs.\ 34.0 tok/s at concurrency~1)
but its tail collapses below the floor by 12 users, while pinned holds $\ge$\SI{10}{tok/s}
to 16. We lose single-stream speed and win concurrency---at high ratio only.

\subsection{Mechanism}
\label{sec:mechanism}
We traced 400 WildChat prompts through OLMoE's router (64 experts per layer, top-8, 16
layers). The concurrency win is the sub-linear growth of the resident set that covers a
fixed fraction of batch traffic; the ~2\ppl\ cap is the routing skew.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{High cross-request overlap.} Median pairwise Jaccard of the activated
        expert sets is $0.593$ (over 19{,}949 pairs), so concurrent users reuse experts
        heavily and a fixed resident set amortises across them---this \emph{is} the win.
  \item \textbf{Sub-linear resident growth.} Experts needed to cover 95\% of a batch's
        traffic grow from $36.4$ (1 user) to $56.2$ (32 users) and $56.7$ (64), of 64; at
        90\% coverage, $31.2$ to $50.7$. Growth is flat because overlap is high.
  \item \textbf{Mild routing skew caps the win.} A \emph{single} request already touches
        $45.2/64 = 70.6\%$ of the pool---there is no tiny hot set to pin, so the benefit
        saturates near $2\ppl$. Per-layer routing is near-uniform (entropy $5.86$ of $6.0$
        bits, mean Zipf $\alpha=0.44$, mean top-10 coverage $28.3\%$).
  \item \textbf{Why it dies on bigger models.} On Qwen3-30B (128 experts $\times$ 48
        layers) the coverage set at useful concurrency exceeds RAM, so the resident set
        cannot amortise and the advantage vanishes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Confounds, stated plainly}
The Qwen1.5-MoE ``loss'' at $1.8\ppl$ mixes the memory-ratio effect with our engine's
lower, noisier per-user throughput at that model's dimensions (60 experts $\times$ 24
layers) plus reduced KV headroom from a ${\sim}$7\,GB resident set. The \emph{direction}
(advantage gone by ${\sim}1.8\ppl$) is robust; the \emph{exact threshold} is not pinned,
because our 4-bit-only engine cannot sweep a single model's footprint cleanly on a 16\,GB
machine. We deliberately do not report a precise crossover ratio.

\paragraph{Strategic implication.} Choose a MoE whose 4-bit footprint is a small
fraction ($\lesssim 1/4$) of unified RAM. At $3.8\ppl$ you roughly double served users;
below ${\sim}2\ppl$ expect no concurrency benefit from pinning.

\section{Experiment II --- Serving Models Bigger Than RAM}
\label{sec:beyond}

\subsection{Setup and subject selection}
We flip to the business-relevant regime: a model larger than device RAM. Subject
selection filters on \emph{active} parameters, not footprint---a $>RAM$ model is only
streamable if few parameters activate per token. We use Qwen3-30B-A3B (18\,GB $>16$\,GB,
$3$B active) as the reference; Mixtral-8x7B (13\,B active) was downloaded, gated
synthetically, and discarded as a poor subject (its active compute makes streaming
unusable on laptop SSD)---kept as a negative data point, not a result.

\subsection{Streaming is a cliff, not a slope}
Holding model, hardware, and engine fixed and varying only the resident-expert fraction
(Table~\ref{tab:cliff}), per-user throughput barely improves from 40\% to 80\% residency
($4.55$, $3.64$, $7.42$ tok/s at 1 user; ceiling~0 in every case) and jumps to $105.7$
tok/s (ceiling~12) only at full residency. The cause is synchronous, serial
cold-load latency: each streamed expert is a blocking disk read. This is the wall any
>RAM system must break.

\centering
\caption{OLMoE pin-fraction sweep, 16\,GB. ``No prefetch'' is serial cold loading;

\subsection{Within-step parallel prefetch turns the cliff into a slope}
Loading a step's cold experts \emph{concurrently}---worker threads read raw
\texttt{safetensors} bytes (thread-safe file I/O) while the main thread performs a
zero-copy view into MLX---converts the cliff into a slope: at 40\% residency the ceiling
rises from 0 to 2 and single-user speed triples. The native router stays authoritative,
so output is byte-identical (SHA unchanged). This is the shipped lever.

\subsection{On the real >RAM model: parallel wins, prediction does not}
\label{sec:abc}
On Qwen3-30B-A3B streamed at pin-fraction~0.1 (13 of 128 experts resident per layer), we
compare three dispatch strategies (Table~\ref{tab:abc}). Within-step \textbf{parallel}
prefetch is a real $2.1\ppl$ win over serial; \textbf{predictive} prefetch
(\emph{cross-step} speculation of the next step's experts) is \emph{worse than serial}.
An earlier report that ``prefetch hurts'' was an implementation bug (a slow
torch$\rightarrow$MLX read with no compute overlap); after rebuilding the read path the
conclusion reverses for parallelism and stands only for prediction.

The reason prediction does not pay on this hardware is bandwidth: with a single SSD,
parallelism already hides the overlap, and a temporal predictor's mispredicted reads
(${\sim}62\%$ miss rate) are wasted traffic that slows the critical path. Budgeting the
speculative queue (per-step caps and a critical-path pause) prevented the worst flooding
but did not make speculation profitable on cacheable models. This is a \emph{careful
negative}, not a refutation of the literature: predictive prefetch should matter when the
working set vastly exceeds cache \emph{and} idle bandwidth exists---a frontier-MoE
regime and multi-NVMe tier we cannot reach on a laptop.

\subsection{Residency-for-KV eviction is refuted}
\label{sec:reskv}
A natural idea is to evict some experts to disk, freeing RAM for more users' KV caches,
and to recover the stream cost with prefetch. On OLMoE under a tight memory budget, this
\emph{lowered} the p05 ceiling from 12 (full residency) to 4 at pin-fractions
$0.5/0.7/0.9$ (Figure~\ref{fig:cliff}). The binding constraint is per-user \emph{speed},
not KV capacity: at full residency the leftover RAM already holds enough KV for ${\sim}12$
users and the speed floor binds first. Do not trade expert residency for KV on a single
consumer SSD; keep experts resident and spend spare RAM on KV.

\subsection{How predictable is routing?}
\label{sec:predict}
To judge whether a learned predictor could help, we measured both aggregate and per-token
predictability on OLMoE. Aggregate: mean top-10 coverage $28.3\%$, mean Zipf $\alpha=0.44$,
entropy $5.86$/$6.0$ bits, top-10 prompt overlap $17.3\%$, cross-layer recall $15.7\%$---
popularity and topic heuristics are weak. Per-token (200 prompts, next-token top-8 recall):
static popularity $0.244$, \emph{temporal} (previous token) $0.381$, \emph{context}
(prompt-so-far) $0.353$, semantic-topic $+0.015$. The exploitable signal is
temporal/contextual, not topical, and a training-free Markov-1 predictor already beats
popularity by ${\sim}14$ recall points---so a learned hidden-state predictor would be an
upgrade, not a prerequisite. Yet even this usable signal did not pay off on a single SSD
(Section~\ref{sec:abc}); it is motivation for testing prediction on higher-bandwidth
hardware, not evidence to deploy it here.

\subsection{Fidelity of streamed inference}
\label{sec:fidelity}
Fidelity rests on three structural gates plus the rental perplexity result
(Section~\ref{sec:gate1ppl}, Table~\ref{tab:ppl}). The synthetic equivalence gate is
\textbf{bit-exact} and the end-to-end greedy match is \textbf{identical (3/3)} on models
that fit RAM; the streaming path reads experts \emph{verbatim} from the same 4-bit slabs,
so numerics are byte-identical by construction and the residual risk is orchestration,
not quantisation. On top of that, the rental gate (post kernel-parity fix) keeps streamed
experts within $0.41\%$ perplexity of resident ones at the sparsest residency we tested.

Two honest caveats remain. First, that gate varied residency on a model that \emph{fits}
memory, so it certifies streamed-equals-resident equivalence, not end-to-end >RAM output
fidelity. Second, a true >RAM cross-engine greedy match (ours-streamed versus a
fully-resident reference) could not be run on the 16\,GB box, because llama.cpp OOMs
before the reference loads; our cross-residency check instead showed \emph{near-tie
rounding divergence}, not bit equality. We therefore treat >RAM token-exact fidelity as
an \emph{open} item, reachable on a larger gate box, and do not claim it here.

\paragraph{Strategic implication.} Big-model-on-small-hardware is, on a single consumer
SSD, a \emph{capability} story bounded by bandwidth (${\approx}\SI{2.4}{tok/s}$ ceiling
at pin~0.1 for a 3B-active model). The next doubling is a hardware tier (parallel NVMe),
not cleverer prediction. The concurrency win of Experiment~I remains the regime where we
beat llama.cpp on served users.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{One mechanism, two regimes.} Experiments~I and~II are the same bounded-working-set
mechanism read at opposite ends of the RAM/model ratio. Where the model is a small
fraction of RAM, the resident set amortises and yields a concurrency win; where the model
approaches and then exceeds RAM, coverage cost and bandwidth dominate, the win vanishes,
and the question becomes feasibility---whether streaming plus I/O overlap can make an
oversized model run at all. The \emph{qs} inequality (arXiv:2603.08960) is the natural
formal frame: routing fragments reuse, and a resident pool that is too large starves KV,
which is exactly why our residency-for-KV experiment backfired.

\paragraph{Position versus the field.} We have implemented pillar~1 of the working-set
synthesis (bounded resident set plus streaming) and measured its ratio dependence. We
have \emph{not} implemented the field's larger levers---properly budgeted predictive
prefetch on fast media, expert-aware token scheduling, or cache-aware routing. Our engine
is therefore close to the \emph{baseline} that the 2025--26 systems improve on, and our
negatives (Sections~\ref{sec:abc}--\ref{sec:fidelity}) are honest statements about what
that baseline does and does not need on one laptop.

\section{Threats to Validity}

\paragraph{Construct.} The p05 ceiling is a proxy for interactive service quality; we
justify it over min/median (Section~\ref{sec:method}) but it is sensitive to the \SI{10}{tok/s}
floor choice.

\paragraph{Internal.} Repetitions are low (a handful per level) for cost reasons; we
re-scored all arms at p05 and moved from min to p05 precisely because the earlier rule
mis-ranked. A custom Metal kernel errored on some shapes and fell back to the validated
\texttt{mx.gather\_qmm} path, so our wins measure \emph{orchestration}, not a bespoke
kernel. We discovered and corrected prose-versus-frozen drifts (bandwidth $9.5\rightarrow
10.6$, >RAM speed $2.32\rightarrow2.12$) and reconciled the perplexity gate to its
post-fix PASS run (the pre-fix FAIL was a caught kernel-parity confound, not the result);
the corrected, frozen values are used throughout.

\paragraph{External.} Only NeoX-uniform-rope, full-attention MoE is supported; Llama-4,
DeepSeek, gpt-oss, Cohere, DBRX, and mamba-hybrids fail pre-flight
(Appendix~B). Hardware is a 16\,GB M1 Pro and one 24\,GB M4; the Qwen3-30B tie is
cross-machine. OS page-cache behaviour inside llama.cpp's mmap path is not directly
controlled.

\paragraph{Conclusion formation.} The crossover \emph{direction} is robust; the exact
threshold ratio is not (Section~\ref{sec:crossover} confound). We claim the former and
disclaim the latter.

\section{Conclusion}

On Apple-Silicon unified memory, pinned-expert orchestration is a
\emph{ratio-conditional} concurrency win and a \emph{bandwidth-bounded} feasibility
mechanism. It doubles served concurrent users for small MoE models that fit comfortably
in RAM (OLMoE, $3.8\ppl$, 16 vs.\ 8), an advantage that vanishes below ${\sim}2\ppl$ and
reverses toward parity as models approach RAM, with the bound explained by measured
routing overlap and skew. Beyond RAM, the same engine turns an unrunnable model into a
runnable one (llama.cpp OOMs; ours streams at ${\approx}\SI{2}{tok/s}$), made usable by
within-step parallel prefetch ($2.1\ppl$) that---unlike predictive prefetch and
residency-for-KV eviction---actually pays on a single consumer SSD. We report the
negatives and the corrected perplexity gate as first-class results. The path from
feasibility to speed runs through a faster storage tier and the scheduling/prefetch
pillars of the recent literature, which our baseline now measures the distance to.

\section{Engine compatibility matrix}
The serve path assumes NeoX (non-traditional) RoPE, full rotary, uniform per-layer
\texttt{self\_attn.rope}, and 4-bit affine experts. \textbf{Compatible:} OLMoE-1B-7B,
Qwen1.5-MoE, Mixtral-8x7B, Qwen3-30B-A3B. \textbf{Fail pre-flight:} LFM2 (conv+attn
hybrid), gpt-oss (partial rotary + sliding window), Llama-4-Scout (traditional rope),
Command-R (traditional rope + sliding window), DeepSeek-V3 (traditional + partial
rotary), DBRX (no \texttt{self\_attn}), Granite-4.0-H (mamba hybrid), EXAONE-MoE
(sliding window, untested). A runtime pre-flight check now fails loudly rather than
producing silently wrong output. The reference product set is therefore the Qwen3-MoE
family plus OLMoE-class models.

\section{The custom-kernel detour (a negative result)}
An early hypothesis was that closing the single-user gap required a bespoke fused gather
kernel. A vectorised Python/MLX \texttt{efficient\_gather\_qmm} reproduced
\texttt{mx.gather\_qmm} to a max difference of $1.4\times10^{-6}$ but ran \textbf{33\ppl
slower} ($22.9$ vs.\ $0.65$\,ms), because it materialises dequantised tensors and cannot
fuse dequantise$+$gather$+$matmul in one kernel the way MLX and llama.cpp do. A
microbenchmark put the standard gather path at $10.6$\,GB/s on the intended-bytes basis
(one run: $9.6$\,GB/s). The takeaway---kernel fusion, not Python-level vectorisation,
governs the per-token floor---redirected the programme from custom kernels to
\emph{orchestration}, which is where our wins come from. This appendix is retained as a
decision-quality case study: the fast, cheap measurement killed an expensive optimisation
before it consumed the project.
